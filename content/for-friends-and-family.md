# I’ve Always Been Misunderstood. The Truth Is, I Didn’t Fully Understand Myself Either.

For much of my life, people have had a hard time figuring me out. I understand why. My career never followed a straight line. I worked more than 60 jobs across very different industries, started businesses that did not become what I believed they could become, changed directions, pushed myself too hard, and pursued ideas that sometimes sounded unrealistic at the time.

From the outside, that can look like instability. For a long time, I sometimes wondered the same thing. It took me decades to understand that there was another pattern underneath all of it: I was constantly learning how systems worked, seeing how different pieces connected, and imagining what those connections could become.

I did not really begin to understand myself until I was around 40. Now, looking backward, I can see how the strengths, mistakes, failures, resilience and experiences of my life eventually prepared me to lead a company built around something I care deeply about: ensuring that increasingly powerful AI remains accountable to the people it affects.

## I Learned How to Work Long Before I Learned How to Understand Myself

I grew up as an only child in Maine, mostly in the countryside. There were not many kids around me, so I spent a lot of time by myself exploring the woods, building things, climbing rocks, skating on ponds and learning to entertain myself. Work was simply part of life. I shoveled a 200-foot driveway by hand, mowed an acre of lawn with a push mower, hauled firewood every winter, and by twelve or thirteen I was helping build a post-and-beam house because I was fascinated by how things went together.

I also grew up in a religious family culture where humility mattered and where believing you were somehow better or smarter than other people was not encouraged. There is value in that lesson, but I think I absorbed something else along with it: be careful about trusting your own abilities too much.

For a kid whose strongest ability may have been seeing connections and possibilities differently from the people around him, that mattered. I learned persistence, responsibility, independence and how to figure things out. What I did not learn was how to recognize my own strengths, trust them, develop them, or surround myself with the people and resources necessary to turn those strengths into something larger.

## 62 Jobs. Thousands of Dots.

Over roughly 30 years, I worked more than 60 jobs. Some were highly technical. Some absolutely were not.

I stocked vitamins for my parents for $3 an hour. I worked construction, farms, restaurants, ski shops and marinas. I worked aboard large yachts. I drafted architecture, HVAC, electrical, plumbing and mechanical systems. I worked around hospitals, semiconductor facilities and major construction projects. I repaired computers and printers, managed technicians, built networks, administered servers, designed websites, programmed automation systems and eventually started companies of my own.

Some of those transitions happened because I was searching. Some happened because I needed money. Some happened because another opportunity interested me more. Some happened because I was pushing myself too hard, always feeling that I needed to prove what I was capable of.

For years, I thought that history meant I had never found where I belonged. Today I see something different.

Steve Jobs once said, **“Creativity is just connecting things.”**

I spent decades collecting things to connect.

Architecture taught me how systems occupy physical space. HVAC taught me dependencies and environmental control. Construction taught me that drawings eventually have to survive reality. Networking taught me that invisible infrastructure determines whether everything above it works. Yachting taught me what happens when complicated systems operate in environments where failures matter. Home automation taught me what changes when software begins controlling the physical world.

Working with tradespeople, technicians, architects, engineers, executives, business owners, wealthy clients and startups taught me something else: different people can look at exactly the same system and see completely different problems.

There was no master plan. I could not have drawn the line connecting those experiences while I was living them. I can see the line now.

## I Started Seeing Patterns Before I Understood What They Meant

By 2001, those accumulated experiences were already changing how I looked at technology. I was not simply interested in individual products. I kept imagining what would happen when different technologies, services and human behaviors were connected.

I did it again in 2013. At the time, I had no objective way to know whether I actually had an unusual ability to see technological convergence early, or whether these were simply more ideas from someone who had spent much of his life searching.

Twenty-five years of hindsight finally gave me a way to find out.

## Seeing It Early Wasn’t Enough

Looking at those documents years later changed how I understood my own history. The problem was not simply that I could not see where technology was going. The record suggests that I often could.

What I did not yet have was everything required to turn foresight into a successful company: capital, mentors, investors, experienced partners, confidence in my own judgment, and enough financial stability to survive while a market caught up.

My solution for much of my life was simpler: work harder, learn more, take on more responsibility and prove myself. When that was not enough, I pushed harder.

That made me resilient. It also meant that I sometimes broke myself down trying to demonstrate a capability I did not feel other people recognized.

One of the things I understand now is that resilience should be the backup system, not the normal operating model. I do not need to keep exhausting myself to prove that I am capable. I need to surround that capability with the right people, systems and resources.

That is one of the biggest differences with SASKI.

# This Time, I Didn’t Just See What Was Coming. I Built for It.

Artificial intelligence is rapidly moving beyond answering questions. AI agents are beginning to access company data, operate software, control infrastructure and perform real-world actions.

That creates enormous opportunity, but it also creates a fundamental problem. AI can reason brilliantly and still misunderstand an instruction, make an incorrect assumption or take an action that should never have been allowed.

SASKI exists to put deterministic governance around those actions.

We allow AI to do what AI is good at: understanding language, reasoning, troubleshooting and proposing actions. SASKI provides an independent governance layer that determines what the AI is actually permitted to do. Actions can be allowed, held for human approval or denied, with an attestation record explaining what happened and why.

SASKI began with safety systems for human-facing AI, especially around children, behavioral health and other vulnerable users. That work has grown into a broader AI governance and attestation platform for enterprise AI agents interacting with physical infrastructure, data and other consequential systems.

Unlike my earlier technology forecasts, SASKI is not something I am waiting for someone else to eventually build.

We built it.

## This Time, I’m Not Alone

This may be the most important difference of all.

For much of my life, I tried to carry ideas largely by myself. I am not doing that with SASKI.

Ishak Kang brings decades of technology-infrastructure experience and direct exposure to major enterprise customers and the manufacturers supplying their systems. Dick Dawson spent more than a decade in management and global business development at Oracle before helping other companies and startups.

Both chose to become cofounders of SASKI. Between them, they bring decades of enterprise technology, infrastructure and sales experience that I do not possess on my own.

They also have something important in the context of this story: they did not grow up with me. They do not carry decades of assumptions about who Stephen is supposed to be.

They looked at the technology, the opportunity and the person building it, and they decided to put their own time, reputations and future upside behind it.

## Why I’m Telling You This Now

I am not writing this because I expect anyone to agree with every decision I have ever made. I have made mistakes. I have pursued things that did not work. I have pushed myself too far. There are parts of my life I would unquestionably handle differently if I had understood then what I understand now.

But I also believe that people close to me have sometimes judged the entire story by the outcomes they could see: another job, another venture, another financial struggle, another idea.

What was much harder to see was the pattern underneath it.

Now there is enough history to evaluate that pattern with evidence.

I am asking the people who know me to separate the person they came to believe I was from the evidence of what I have actually seen, learned, built and accomplished. I am not asking anyone to believe in an old version of me or even to take my word for who I am today. I am asking you to look at the evidence and decide for yourself.

The timing matters because there is an uncomfortable irony in where I am today. SASKI has become more technically mature, more valuable and surrounded by more experienced people than at any point since I started it, while I am personally under enormous financial pressure from spending more than a year bootstrapping the company.

SASKI itself continues moving forward. We have working technology, multiple products, experienced partners, enterprise conversations, manufacturer interest and opportunities developing across several industries. We were also accepted into the FDA TEMPO program for research involving behavioral-health AI systems.

I do not need help because I have lost confidence in what I am building. I need help because I believe in it more strongly than I ever have, and I need enough runway to cross the distance between building the technology and generating meaningful recurring revenue from it.

## Maybe I Wasn’t Failing to Find My Place

For most of my life, I thought I was failing to find where I belonged. I moved through industries, jobs, businesses and ideas looking for something I could never quite define.

Now I understand that those experiences were not as disconnected as they looked. I was learning how physical systems, digital systems, businesses and people interact. I was collecting the pieces that I would eventually learn to connect.

SASKI may be the first place where all of those pieces converge.

Perhaps I needed to understand my own intelligence, weaknesses, resilience and values before I was ready to lead a company whose purpose is, in part, to put boundaries around intelligence itself.

SASKI is built around a simple belief: intelligence alone is not enough. It needs boundaries, accountability and evidence. And when commercial pressure and human safety come into conflict, people have to come first.

That is the company I am building.

And, finally, I understand why I am the person building it.
